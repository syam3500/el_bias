Quantification bias in E&L screening: how the uncertainty factor works

In non-targeted screening, extractables are quantified against one surrogate standard. Compounds that respond less than the surrogate (RRF < 1) are underestimated, and some of them fall below the analytical evaluation threshold (AET) although their true concentration is above it. The uncertainty factor (UF) lowers the AET to compensate for this.

This site explains three things from our study: where the ISO 10993-18 UF formula comes from and why it fails, what the Monte Carlo simulation did, and how to calculate a UF from your own RRF data.

1. Where the UF formula comes from

ISO 10993-18 (Amendment 1, 2022) gives UF = 1/(1 − RSD), where RSD is the relative standard deviation of the response factors in a reference database. The standard does not give a derivation. Below, the UF is derived from the definition of a false negative. The derivation shows which assumptions the ISO formula needs, and that one term is missing.

  1. Measurement modelWith a single surrogate, the estimated concentration is the true concentration multiplied by the compound's relative response factor:
    Cest = Ctrue × RRF
    A compound with RRF = 0.2 is reported at one fifth of its true concentration.
  2. Decision ruleLet T be the true AET (no UF applied). The laboratory reports a compound when its estimated concentration reaches the lowered threshold T* = T / UF. So, by definition:
    UF = TT*
  3. False negativeA false negative (FN) is a compound that is truly at or above the AET but is not reported:
    PFN = P( Ctrue × RRF < T*  |  Ctrue ≥ T )
  4. Worst caseAmong the compounds that must be reported, the one most likely to be missed sits exactly at the AET (Ctrue = T). Any compound above T is easier to detect. This gives an upper bound, where F is the cumulative distribution function of the RRFs:
    PFN ≤ P( T × RRF < T* ) = P( RRF < 1UF ) = F( 1UF )
    In words: a compound at the AET is missed when its RRF is lower than 1/UF.
  5. Choose an acceptable FN risk βRequiring F(1/UF) ≤ β and applying the quantile function F−1 gives the general formula (Eq. 3 in the paper):
    UF = 1F−1(β)percentile UF
    F−1(β) is the β-quantile of the RRFs: the value below which a fraction β of the reference compounds fall. No distribution is assumed. The formula only needs F−1(β) > 0, which means that less than β×100 % of the reference compounds may be non-detects.
  6. Add a normal assumptionIf the RRFs follow a normal distribution N(μ, σ²), then F−1(β) = μ + σ Φ−1(β). For β = 0.16, Φ−1(0.16) ≈ −1, so the 16th percentile is about μ − σ. With RSD = σ/μ (Eq. 4 in the paper):
    UF = 1μ − σ = 1μ (1 − RSD)UF(μRSD)
  7. Compare with ISO 10993-18The ISO formula is the same expression without μ (Eq. 5):
    UFISO = 11 − RSD
    The two are equal only when the mean RRF μ is 1.

Three conditions behind the ISO formula

The derivation shows that 1/(1 − RSD) gives the intended 16 % protection only if all of the following hold:

μ ≈ 1Laboratories choose a strongly responding surrogate (for example 2-fluorobiphenyl) so that most extractables have RRF < 1. As a result μ is usually below 1, and the ISO UF is too small. For the same 31 GC/MS compounds measured against p-terphenyl-d14 (μ = 0.473, RSD = 0.417), UFISO = 1.72 while UF(μRSD) = 3.63. When μ > 1 the ISO UF is too large instead.
RSD < 1When RSD ≥ 1 the denominator is zero or negative, and no UF can be calculated. The 2022 amendment states this limit. All three FDA CLAP datasets are outside it: RSD = 1.24 (GC/MS), 2.15 (LC/MS POS) and 2.24 (LC/MS NEG) at the 5 µg/mL surrogate level. Published LC/MS databases give 1.08 to 2.72.
Approximately normal RRFsThe step from the 16th percentile to μ − σ needs a normal distribution. CLAP RRFs are right-skewed, with many low values and a few very high ones. For skewed data, the percentile formula of step 5 should be used directly.

2. What the Monte Carlo simulation did

The derivation gives a bound for one compound at the AET. A real screen contains many compounds at very different concentrations. The simulation measures how often each correction method misses a compound (FN rate) and how often it reports one that is truly below the AET (FP rate) across a full study.

One iteration

  1. True concentrations80 true concentrations are drawn from a log-normal distribution fitted to the extractables data of Nahan et al. (2020): ln Ctrue ~ N(−2.4, 4.6²) for GC/MS and N(−2.3, 4.8²) for LC/MS.
  2. RRFsEach compound gets an RRF. GC/MS: 80 of the 92 CLAP values, drawn without replacement. LC/MS POS and NEG: draws from a gamma distribution fitted to CLAP by censored maximum likelihood, with non-detects kept as left-censored values.
  3. MeasurementThe estimated concentration is Cest = Ctrue × RRF.
  4. CorrectionCest is corrected with one of the methods (multiplied by a UF, or divided by the compound's own measured RRF for the conditional methods).
  5. ClassificationEach compound is compared with the true AET: truly above or not, reported above or not. This gives true positives (TP), false negatives (FN), false positives (FP) and true negatives (TN).
  6. RepetitionSteps 1 to 5 are repeated 10,000 times and the counts are pooled: FN rate = FN / (TP + FN), FP rate = FP / (FP + TN).

One batch of 80 compounds

Each point is one compound. Its position on the horizontal axis is the true concentration, on the vertical axis the corrected concentration. Compounds to the right of the vertical line must be reported. Compounds above the horizontal line are reported. The lower-right corner holds the false negatives.

Axes are logarithmic and cut at 0.0001 and 10,000 µg/mL; points outside are drawn on the edge. Non-detects (RRF = 0) have Cest = 0 and are drawn on the bottom edge. Under the conditional methods a non-detect gives 0/0 and is left out, as in the original R code.

What the simulation showed

Without correction, many compounds above the AET are missedAt AET = 0.45 µg/mL the FN rate was 40.7 % (GC/MS), 42.2 % (LC/MS POS) and 81.9 % (LC/MS NEG).

The ISO formula cannot be used on the CLAP dataBecause RSD > 1 for every technique, UF(RSD) and UF(μRSD) are negative, and every compound is classified below the AET (FN = 100 %). These two rows are shown only as an out-of-domain reference, not as an ISO-compliant use.

Fixed UFs trade FN for FPFor GC/MS, moving from UF = 2 to UF = 10 lowered FN from 32.1 % to 21.3 % and raised FP from 2.1 % to 12.5 %. For LC/MS NEG, UF = 10 still missed 72.5 % of the compounds above the AET.

Percentile UFs keep FN at or below βThe 10th-percentile UF held FN near 10 % for GC/MS and below 10 % for both LC/MS modes, for every AET and concentration model. The price is a very large UF wherever the target percentile falls inside the non-detected fraction (UF > 999).

Conditional corrections reach about 2 % FNRRFlow and RRF < 1 gave FN rates near 2 %. This result assumes that every compound is identified and has a measured RRF, which is rarely true in routine screening. Read it as a ceiling for the identified, responding compounds.

How to read the verdictEstimated AET = true AET / UF. Feasible: estimated AET ≥ LOQ. Borderline: between LOD and LOQ. Nullified: below LOD, so every peak the instrument sees must be reported and the AET no longer works as a filter. If the β you want is not feasible, the recommended UF is capped at AET/LOQ, and the calculator reports the FN risk that this cap delivers.
β = 5 % is a starting value, not a result of the simulation. Choosing β is a documented risk decision for your laboratory. A UF only describes the dataset it comes from (compounds, instrument, internal standard, day of analysis). If you use CLAP data, check that they represent your own system.

Data and limits

RRF dataUS FDA Chemicals List for Analytical Performance (CLAP), 106 reference standards: 92 measured by GC/MS, 57 by LC/MS POS and 28 by LC/MS NEG, at surrogate concentrations of 5, 10 and 20 µg/mL. Non-detects are coded 0 and values below the detection limit 0.001. Non-detected fractions: 10.9 % (GC/MS), 19.3 % (LC/MS POS), 60.7 % (LC/MS NEG).

Browser simulationA JavaScript translation of the R code in scripts/simulation/ (log-normal fitted concentration scenario). It uses the same distributions and the UF values applied in the paper, but a different random number generator, so results differ slightly from the published tables. In our check over all 36 settings, the median difference was 0.05 percentage points for FN. The same random draws are used for every method, so differences between methods come from the method only. The machine-learning correction is not included, because it needs the trained Random Forest model, and its published result is inflated by information leakage.

Model limitsThe simulation assumes a linear response, RRFs that do not depend on concentration, independent compounds, a concentration profile from one study and batches of 80 compounds. The confidence intervals in the paper describe Monte Carlo error only. The conditional methods assume complete identification and a reliable compound-specific RRF.
